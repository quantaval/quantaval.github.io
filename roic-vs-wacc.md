# When Growth Pays: Reading the Gap Between ROIC and WACC

*Two companies can grow revenue at 20% and deserve completely different valuations. The difference is not the growth rate — it is what each new dollar of capital earns against what that capital costs.*

Start with the uncomfortable case first. A business that earns 6% on its capital while capital costs 9% is destroying value with every dollar it reinvests — no matter how fast its revenue grows. A business that earns 15% while capital costs 9% is creating value with every dollar it reinvests. The spread between return on invested capital (ROIC) and the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) is the cleanest single test of whether growth is worth having.

## The idea in one paragraph

ROIC measures what a business earns on the money tied up in it: net operating profit after tax divided by invested capital. WACC measures what that money costs: the blended return debt and equity holders require. When ROIC exceeds WACC, each new dollar invested earns more than it costs, and the business is worth more than the capital put into it. When ROIC is below WACC, growth consumes value. A company can report record revenue and record profit and still be worth less than the capital it consumed to get there.

## A worked example first

Take a hypothetical company, Example Industrial Co. — invented for teaching only, not a real company.

- Net operating profit after tax (NOPAT): $120 million
- Invested capital: $800 million
- Cost of equity: 10%, cost of debt after tax: 4%
- Capital structure: 60% equity, 40% debt

ROIC = $120m ÷ $800m = **15.0%**

WACC = (60% × 10%) + (40% × 4%) = 6.0% + 1.6% = **7.6%**

Spread = 15.0% − 7.6% = **+7.4 percentage points**

Every dollar this business reinvests earns 15 cents against a cost of 7.6 cents. Growth adds value here. Now flip the numbers: if NOPAT were $48 million instead, ROIC would be 6.0% — below the 7.6% cost of capital — and every dollar of growth would destroy about 1.6 cents of value. Same revenue growth, opposite conclusion.

## The formulas behind the example

> ROIC = NOPAT ÷ Invested Capital
>
> WACC = (E/V × Re) + (D/V × Rd × (1 − Tax Rate))
>
> Economic Profit = (ROIC − WACC) × Invested Capital

## Why the spread matters more than the growth rate

Growth is only good when the return on the new capital beats its cost. This is why two companies growing revenue at 20% can deserve completely different valuations: one funds growth at 15% ROIC, the other at 5%. The market eventually prices the spread, not the growth rate.

The spread also explains the moat. A business that sustains ROIC well above WACC for a decade is usually protected — brand, scale, switching costs, network effects. A spread that fades toward WACC is a moat eroding. ## When ROIC vs WACC fails

- **Asset-light businesses.** Software and services can show enormous ROIC because the balance sheet misses the real investment — R&D and sales spending expensed rather than capitalised. Compare within the same industry, not across it.
- **Cyclical peaks and troughs.** A peak-year NOPAT flatters ROIC; a trough-year NOPAT understates it. Normalise earnings across the cycle before judging.
- **WACC is an estimate, not a fact.** The cost of equity depends on a beta and an equity risk premium that are both arguable. Move WACC by one point and the spread moves with it — always show the sensitivity.
- **Accounting distortions.** Heavy [buybacks](https://quantaval.com/buyback-program) shrink equity and inflate returns; large intangibles sitting off the balance sheet break the link between capital and earning power. Read the capital definition before trusting the number.

The equity-side cousin, [return on equity](https://quantaval.com/roe-calculator), is easier still to flatter — its DuPont breakdown shows exactly where leverage and buybacks enter the number.

## How to use it in practice

1. Compute ROIC and WACC for the company — and for its peers, on the same definitions.
2. Look at [the spread's trend over five to ten years](https://quantaval.com/equity-research), not one year.
3. Ask where [reinvestment](https://quantaval.com/sales-to-capital) is going: is the company putting new capital into the high-ROIC core, or into low-return side bets?
4. Cross-check with other models. A wide spread supports a higher multiple; a narrow or negative spread argues for a lower one.
